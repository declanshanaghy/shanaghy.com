The Plain Facts, Carved

What It Is

ᚅ

Arduino libraries for the chips that turned up again and again across the projects — the garden’s early bench, written down. Driver code for the LED drivers, the port expanders, the sensors and relays that every 2011–2013 build seemed to need: written once, kept where every later build could reach them.

Each library is C++ in the Arduino tradition — a class, a begin() method, a handful of calls that make a strange chip feel like an old friend. They were forged the hard way, chip by chip, the way libraries always are: in the middle of a project that needed the chip yesterday.

They exist because the young maker in Athlone learned what every maker learns — that you build the tool first, and the thing second.

Told in the Seanchaí Tradition

The Build Story

ᚏ

Ogma, the honey-mouthed god of the Irish, looked at ink on a skin and was not impressed. Ink fades, skins burn, floods carry pages to the sea — so Ogma cut his letters into stone instead, and named the cutting ogham, and ogham’s whole grammar is edges: a stroke for the ridge of a stone, a notch for its corner. A thing carved once is read by everything after. The Athlone workshop learned the same sermon around 2012, when the bench was a desk, the solder iron was new, and every project kept reaching for the same half-dozen chips — the LED driver, the expander, the relay — and kept writing the same forty lines to wake each one up. The young maker did what Ogma did: stopped, cut it into a library, and let the stone do the remembering. After that the chips answered by name. One of those cuts is on film — an LED rig failing beautifully on the bench while the library held — and the scar it left grew moss. Write once, in stone, said the bench. Everything after will read you.
