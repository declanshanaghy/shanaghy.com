The Plain Facts, Carved

What It Is

ᚅ

A breakout board for a MAX756 — the SMPS step-up converter, given legs. Two hungry cells at the input, a civilized rail at the output, and the inductor screaming ultrasonically in between: a small board that lifts what it is given.

The MAX756 is the modern bench’s answer to the old problem — the batteries are low but the circuit is proud — and the breakout makes it usable: the part broken out to solderable pads, the feedback and the switch brought where a human can reach them, the whole spell cast in copper.

It exists because step-up converters are the most useful magic in the parts drawer, and magic you cannot breadboard is magic you cannot use.

ᚏ

Told in the Seanchaí Tradition

The Build Story

ᚏ

The smiths of the old stories were not valued for strength; they were valued for thrift. The tale of Goibniu or Luchtaine always turns on the same quiet marvel: nothing wasted — the fire raised to exactly what the work needed, not a coal more, the heat lifted out of a low flame as if the flame had never heard that it was too small. And Brigid’s bellows, the ones the church inherited and never explained, had the same reputation: a modest fire, a working heat, no coal spent on show. The MAX756 is that reputation in an eight-pin part. You give it two fading batteries, the kind every drawer is full of, and it does the smith’s trick — lifts them, switching faster than the ear can hear, into a rail that behaves as if the batteries were new. In 2013 the bench broke the spell out onto its own board so it could be used — because a converter in a package is a marvel, but a converter on a breakout is a tool. Raise the small fire, says the smithcraft, and waste nothing telling anyone it was small. The moss grows along the trace, green over the copper the way thrift grows over anything patient.
