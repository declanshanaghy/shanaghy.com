The Plain Facts, Carved

What It Is

ᚅ

A keypad backpack: a small board that straps a matrix keypad to an Arduino’s back and teaches it manners — a C++ library so the rows and columns come back as numbers instead of pins. The keypress goes in, the character comes out, and the wiring is the backpack’s problem, not the build’s.

Keypads are dirt-simple and secretly tedious: scan a row, read a column, debounce the bounce, and do it again fast enough that a human’s finger never feels the polling. The backpack does all of it behind the strap, and the main sketch just asks for input and gets it.

It exists because every dolmen knows its own passcode — and every workshop eventually builds something that needs one.

Told in the Seanchaí Tradition

The Build Story

ᚏ

In the old stories there are two kinds of door in a rath, and the difference is the whole of politics: there is the door that keeps everyone out, and there is the door that knows its own people — the souterrain’s slab that swings for the household and grinds for the stranger, not because it is stronger, but because somewhere in the dark ahead of it a voice was taught which knock to give. The passcode is the modern form of the second door: the lock that opens to the knuckles it knows. The trouble with the modern form is that the keypad, on its own, is a foreigner — rows and columns, blind, mute, sending its little grid of secrets to whoever will interpret. So in 2012 the Athlone bench built the backpack: a small board riding between the keys and the brain, speaking C++ politely on one side and grid on the other, so the lock could finally know its own people without the main project having to learn the whole grammar of the door each time. The door doesn’t judge, says the old story. The one who taught it does. And it wrote up the teaching, and the moss grew on the strap like the green on a well-handled latch.
