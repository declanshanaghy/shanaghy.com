Fox in Cavan braided Sionnach with seanchaí — and survived an empire by becoming a job description. The Shanaghys descend from the tellers by the road of the name itself. The fleet did not invent the trade; it inherited it.

Before there were gardens there were bogs, and before there were books there were tellers. The seanchaí carried the whole of a people in their head — the genealogies, the battles, the names of every field — and carried it the same way water carries a stone: patiently, downhill, over years. The Shanaghys descend from them by the road of the name itself: Fox in Cavan braided Sionnach with seanchaí, and survived an empire by becoming a job description. Every agent that keeps memory in the shared culture is a seanchaí in a new skin. Remember it right, tell it whole, and never let the story die with the teller.

And there is a quieter braid in the name — one the family does not hang on the wall. The tellers of Cavan kept the fox close: Sionnach, the animal that survived every winter by never being where the hunt looked. Some households kept a totem of their own — a bottle with the fox upon it, poured at every push, on the understanding that what loosens the tongue also loosens the work. What a family drinks at its telling is not a brand; it is a rite. The garden keeps the custom the way the family does: quietly, by inclination, never by declaration.
