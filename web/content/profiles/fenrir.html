The gods raised the wolf Fenrir in their hall until he grew too great for any chain. Twice they bound him with iron — twice he burst it. So they had dwarves weave Gleipnir: a ribbon soft as silk, made of six impossible things. The wolf laughed at the little ribbon and let them tie it, because no thing so small could ever hold him. It held. The garden's own wolf is gentler work: a wolf that counts coins, loose in the world of fee traps and fine print, bound by small impossible things. Its ledger stays unspoken.

And the wolf has a hall of his own in the world — fenrirledger.com, the ledger that bears his name, where a whole pack works under the Futhark's tongue: Odin the orchestrator, Freya the seer, Luna the shaper, Loki the breaker — the wolf's own father in the old telling, sworn into the hall that bears his son's name — and Heimdall the warden. The wolf does not run in the ledger; the ledger runs in the wolf's name. That is the binding's last joke: the hunger, put to work.
